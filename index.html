About the tool

From plain English to publication-ready charts

The Religion & Politics Explorer is a research tool developed at WashU's Data, Technology, and Research Center. Researchers type a question in plain English — "How has abortion support changed among Republicans since 1980?" — and the tool generates a survey-weighted chart with confidence intervals, drawn directly from microdata.

No SQL. No data wrangling. No coding. The platform handles variable selection, survey weighting, and statistical aggregation automatically, letting researchers focus on interpretation rather than data preparation.

1

Ask a question

Type any research question about U.S. religion, politics, or social attitudes. The tool understands follow-up questions and context from prior turns.

2

Review the analysis

The tool explains what it found in plain language, shows the SQL used to query the data, and flags small sample sizes automatically.

3

Explore and export

Customize colors, fonts, and confidence bands. Download as CSV, PNG, or interactive HTML for embedding in publications and newsletters.

Data sources

Two of the most comprehensive surveys of American public opinion

All charts are generated from raw microdata — individual survey responses with survey weights applied — not pre-aggregated tables. This gives researchers full flexibility to slice by any combination of variables.

GSS

General Social Survey

Run by NORC at the University of Chicago since 1972, the GSS is the gold standard for tracking long-run trends in American social attitudes. It covers religion, politics, race, gender, sexuality, and more.

Coverage: 1972 – 2024 · Biennial · ~2,000 respondents/year
Key variables: Party ID, abortion, church attendance, race, education, ideology
CES

Cooperative Election Study

Formerly CCES, the CES is a large-scale annual survey fielded since 2006, with sample sizes of 50,000+ respondents per year. Its scale makes it ideal for state-level and subgroup analysis.

Coverage: 2006 – 2024 · Annual · 50,000+ respondents/year
Key variables: Party ID, religion, ideology, vote choice, state, race
